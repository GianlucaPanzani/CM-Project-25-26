% Comparison: QR vs.\ L-BFGS
\label{sec:comparison}
% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\noindent The preceding chapters studied L-BFGS and structure-based
Householder QR separately. We now compare them on the same problems and
under a common timing protocol, considering both computational cost and
achieved numerical accuracy. The experiments cover the initial ML-CUP25
instance, dimensional scaling, regularization, a prescribed difficult
spectrum, and reuse of a QR factorization for several right-hand sides.
A thin-SVD solver also provides an external runtime baseline.
All numerical values and figures in this chapter come from the saved
outputs of \texttt{04\_comparison.ipynb}; figure filenames identify their
source notebook sections.

\section{Experimental Protocol and Accuracy Measures}
\label{sec:comparison_protocol}

\noindent The initial problem uses the standardized ML-CUP25 matrix with
$m=500$, $n=12$, $\lambda=0.5$, and the same synthetic response vector as
Chapter~\ref{sec:experiments} (seed 42). Accuracy therefore concerns the
numerical solution of the regularized least-squares problem, rather than
prediction on unseen data. Unless stated otherwise, L-BFGS starts from
$w_0=0$, uses memory $\bar m=12$, BB1 scaling, exact line search, no
curvature-based restart, relative gradient tolerance $10^{-14}$, and a
budget of 2000 iterations. The curvature-pair acceptance safeguard remains
active. The two exceptions to the memory setting are stated explicitly:
$\bar m=\max(12,n)$ in the $n$ sweep and $\bar m=24$ in the spectral stress
test.

\noindent For each timed configuration, \texttt{benchmark\_solvers} performs
one discarded warm-up per method and ten measured repetitions, shuffling
method order with a fixed seed. An external timer surrounds each complete
solver call, including initialization, factorization and back substitution
where applicable. Normal solver histories and reflector storage are also
included. Data construction, the numerical reference, and common accuracy
diagnostics are outside the timed regions. We report medians and
interquartile ranges (IQRs); the latter describe timing dispersion, not
confidence intervals. The saved run uses double precision, Python 3.13.9,
NumPy 2.1.3, and one OpenBLAS thread on an ARM64 machine. These timings
should not be directly substituted for the internal solver timings in
Chapters~\ref{sec:experiments} and~\ref{sec:qr_exp}.

\noindent The thin-SVD solution of Eq.~\eqref{eq:qr_svd_reference} supplies
the common reference $w_{\mathrm{SVD}}$. The main accuracy measures are
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:comparison_gradient}
    e_w=\frac{\norm{w-w_{\mathrm{SVD}}}_2}{\norm{w_{\mathrm{SVD}}}_2},
    \qquad
    g_{\mathrm{rel}}=
    \frac{\norm{X(X^Tw-y)+\lambda^2w}_2}{\norm{Xy}_2}.
\end{equation}
The forward error agrees with Eq.~\eqref{eq:qr_forward_error}, while the
relative gradient uses the stopping normalization of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:stop_rel}, since $\nabla f(0)=-Xy$. Its denominator differs
from that of $\eta_{\mathrm{opt}}$ in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:qr_scaled_optimality}; their numerical values are therefore
not interchangeable. QR and SVD are labelled as direct solves and are
assessed through the same metrics, without assigning them an iterative
stopping tolerance.

\noindent The notebook also checks fitted-vector discrepancies, data and
augmented residuals, and scaled stationarity. Objective agreement alone
can conceal coefficient errors. In particular, with
$e=w-w_{\mathrm{SVD}}$ and $H=XX^T+\lambda^2I_m$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:comparison_energy}
    f(w)-f(w_{\mathrm{SVD}})
    =\nabla f(w_{\mathrm{SVD}})^Te+\tfrac12e^THe.
\end{equation}
The nonnegative quantity $e^THe/(2f(w_{\mathrm{SVD}}))$ can be evaluated
without subtracting nearly equal objectives, but equals the relative
objective gap only at an exact reference minimizer. The SVD reference is
itself computed in floating point and has
$g_{\mathrm{rel}}\approx2.0\times10^{-15}$ on the initial instance.

\subsection{Time at a Common Accuracy Requirement}
\label{sec:comparison_quality}

\noindent Figure~\ref{fig:comparison_time_accuracy} compares complete
L-BFGS runs at tolerances from $10^{-2}$ to $10^{-14}$ with the direct QR
result. Tightening the tolerance first improves both coefficient accuracy
and optimality, at the expense of additional iterations. The three
tightest tolerances all reach the same 12-iteration solution; their points
nearly coincide and their timing differences reflect measurement
variability. All sampled L-BFGS runs converge, so no measured point uses
the failure marker shown in the legend. QR supplies a final solution,
rather than an iterative trajectory.

\noindent At the common requirements $e_w\leq10^{-6}$ and
$g_{\mathrm{rel}}\leq10^{-8}$, the fastest sampled qualifying L-BFGS run
requests $\varepsilon=10^{-8}$. It takes 10 iterations and $0.409$ ms,
with $e_w=1.41\times10^{-7}$ and $g_{\mathrm{rel}}=8.70\times10^{-9}$.
QR also meets both targets, taking $11.42$ ms, so the measured time ratio
is approximately $27.9$. L-BFGS can therefore reduce its cost when full
working-precision accuracy is unnecessary. This comparison uses common
acceptance thresholds, rather than identical achieved errors or globally
optimal parameter tuning.

\section{Dimensional Scaling and Storage}
\label{sec:comparison_scaling}

\subsection{Increasing the Number of Unknowns}

\noindent The first dimension sweep follows the nested row-resampling
construction of Section~\ref{sec:qr_scalability}. Original rows are retained,
additional rows are sampled with small Gaussian perturbations, and prefixes
give $m\in\{100,250,500,1000,2000,4000\}$ at fixed $n=12$ and $\lambda=0.5$.
Conditioning can change with $m$, unlike the prescribed-spectrum $m$ study
in Section~\ref{sec:exp_scaling_nm}.

\noindent Figure~\ref{fig:comparison_scaling_m} shows a growing timing
advantage for L-BFGS while both methods retain coefficient errors below
$10^{-14}$. L-BFGS takes 12 iterations at every sampled size, with time
increasing from $0.469$ to $1.799$ ms; QR increases from $1.987$ to
$231.5$ ms. The QR/L-BFGS ratio consequently grows from about $4.2$ at
$m=100$ to $128.7$ at $m=4000$. The middle and right panels show that this
advantage is obtained without sacrificing convergence or forward accuracy.

\noindent These observations agree with the cost models of
Sections~\ref{sec:exp_cost} and~\ref{sec:qr_flop_count}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:comparison_cost}
    T_{\mathrm{L\text{-}BFGS}}=O\!\left(k(mn+\bar m\,m)\right),
    \qquad
    T_{\mathrm{QR}}=O\!\left((n+1)m^2\right).
\end{equation}
Here $k$ is the observed number of iterations. At fixed $n$, $\bar m$,
and comparable $k$, L-BFGS has linear leading work in $m$, whereas the
structured QR factorization retains a quadratic term. The dashed guides
are normalized at the smallest size; finite-size overheads mean that the
measured curves need not follow them exactly.

\subsection{The Thin-SVD Runtime Baseline}
\label{sec:comparison_svd_runtime}

\noindent A second timing sweep adds the thin-SVD solver to assess the
cost of both project implementations against a library-based baseline.
All three methods are remeasured together, with the same external timer
and randomized order; these are separate measurements from
Figure~\ref{fig:comparison_scaling_m}. Every QR and SVD call includes a
fresh factorization. The timed SVD routine computes only its factors and
the regularized solution of Eq.~\eqref{eq:qr_svd_reference}; reference
norms, condition numbers, and error diagnostics remain outside the timer.

\noindent Figure~\ref{fig:comparison_svd_runtime} shows that SVD is the
fastest of the three at every sampled size. At $m=500$, its median is
$0.0950$ ms, compared with $0.584$ ms for L-BFGS and $11.26$ ms for QR.
At $m=4000$, the corresponding values are $0.625$, $1.908$, and $237.8$ ms.
Thus the superiority of L-BFGS over the project QR implementation does not
make it the fastest available solver for this particular matrix shape.
For $m>n$, thin SVD costs $O(mn^2+n^3)$ and operates directly on the tall,
narrow matrix $X$, whereas the structured QR solver still constructs an
$m\times m$ triangular factor. The compiled SVD implementation also has
different execution overheads from the project routines. Both the
algorithmic structure and the implementation contribute to this ranking.

\noindent The SVD forward discrepancy against the SVD reference is zero
in the saved table, as expected from comparing the same formula and
factorization routine. This is not an independent accuracy certificate;
its relative gradient, between approximately $1.3\times10^{-15}$ and
$3.4\times10^{-15}$, supplies an additional optimality check. The comparison
therefore gives a useful runtime baseline while preserving the distinction
between reference construction and measured solver work.

\subsection{Increasing the Number of Spectral Directions}
\label{sec:comparison_n_scaling}

\noindent The $n$ sweep fixes $m=500$ and $\lambda=0.5$, and uses the
prescribed-spectrum construction of Section~\ref{sec:exp_scaling_nm}.
The singular values are geometrically spaced from $75.74$ to $75.74/20$,
so $\kappa_2(A)\approx151.5$ remains constant. We test
$n\in\{5,12,25,50,100,150,200,250,300\}$ and set
$\bar m=\max(12,n)$. This experiment increases both the number of spectral
directions and the available L-BFGS memory; it is not a fixed-memory test.

\noindent In Figure~\ref{fig:comparison_scaling_n}, L-BFGS is much faster
at small $n$, but its advantage decreases as both the iteration count and
the two-loop work grow. The iteration counts are
$6,12,25,50,93,122,146,167,183$, respectively, and all runs meet the
requested tolerance. At $n=100$, L-BFGS takes $18.65$ ms against $27.53$ ms
for QR. At $n=200$ the medians are nearly equal ($45.58$ and $44.98$ ms);
by $n=300$, QR has the lower median, $62.36$ ms against $72.63$ ms.
These values locate a crossover within the tested range rather than a
universal threshold, particularly given the variability near the crossing.

\noindent The accuracy panel also changes with dimension. At $n=300$,
L-BFGS and QR give $e_w=5.42\times10^{-14}$ and
$2.26\times10^{-14}$, respectively. Both remain accurate, while QR has
smaller forward errors over the larger-$n$ cases. Since memory increases
with $n$, the loss of L-BFGS's timing advantage cannot be attributed to a
fixed history that is too short. More iterations and more stored pairs
both contribute. The near-$n$ behavior at smaller dimensions is consistent
with the subspace discussion in Section~\ref{sec:finite_termination}, but
does not constitute a finite-termination guarantee for this implementation.

\subsection{Principal Storage Requirements}
\label{sec:comparison_storage}

\noindent Figure~\ref{fig:comparison_storage} complements the runtime
comparison with an analytical count of the principal stored arrays. In
double precision, the capacity of the L-BFGS pair history is
$8(2\bar m\,m)$ bytes; the dense array for $R$ and the compact QR reflectors
require $8(m^2+m(n+1))$ bytes. At $m=4000$, $n=12$, and $\bar m=12$, these
counts are $0.732$ MiB and $122.467$ MiB, respectively, a factor of about
167. This makes the linear-versus-quadratic storage distinction relevant
even when both solvers attain essentially the same coefficient accuracy.

\noindent These curves exclude shared inputs, temporary arrays, Python
objects, and convergence histories. They are not measurements of peak RAM,
and the L-BFGS curve describes pair capacity rather than necessarily
occupied storage. The fixed $\bar m=12$ model applies to the $m$ sweep;
it should not be transferred unchanged to the growing-memory $n$ sweep.

\section{Regularization and Spectral Difficulty}
\label{sec:comparison_conditioning}

\subsection{Regularization on ML-CUP25}

\noindent Figure~\ref{fig:comparison_regularization} varies $\lambda$
on the same ML-CUP25 data. Each value defines a different minimizer and
uses its own SVD reference. For small $\lambda$, the runtime panel changes
little: L-BFGS still takes 12 iterations, whereas QR always performs a
complete factorization. At stronger regularization the iteration count
falls to 9 at $\lambda=10$ and 6 at $\lambda=100$, reducing the L-BFGS time
further.

\noindent The forward-error and gradient panels reveal a different aspect
of the comparison. At $\lambda=10^{-8}$, QR gives
$e_w=7.70\times10^{-8}$ despite $g_{\mathrm{rel}}=5.59\times10^{-16}$;
L-BFGS gives $e_w=2.08\times10^{-15}$ with
$g_{\mathrm{rel}}=2.22\times10^{-16}$. Thus a tiny optimality residual
can coexist with a much larger coefficient error. QR avoids explicitly
forming the normal equations, as explained in Chapter~\ref{sec:qr}, but
its forward accuracy still depends on the conditioning and geometry of
the least-squares problem.

\noindent The conditioning panel explains why the full condition number
alone does not predict L-BFGS behavior from zero. In exact arithmetic the
iterates remain in $\operatorname{range}(X)$, where, for full-column-rank $X$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:comparison_restricted_condition}
    \kappa_2\!\left(H|_{\operatorname{range}(X)}\right)
    =\frac{\sigma_{\max}(X)^2+\lambda^2}
           {\sigma_{\min}(X)^2+\lambda^2}.
\end{equation}
This ratio remains about $10^3$ at small $\lambda$, while the full Hessian
has additional eigenvalues $\lambda^2$ and reaches a condition number of
about $5.7\times10^{19}$ at $\lambda=10^{-8}$. This distinction is consistent
with the stable L-BFGS forward errors in the plot. The precise iteration
counts remain empirical, subject to scaling, curvature safeguards, and
rounding. Nor is L-BFGS uniformly more accurate: at $\lambda=10$, its
forward error is $5.05\times10^{-14}$ against $6.24\times10^{-15}$ for QR,
although it meets the requested gradient tolerance.

\subsection{A Difficult Prescribed Spectrum}
\label{sec:comparison_stress}

\noindent The stress test fixes $m=160$, $n=24$, and geometrically spaced
singular values from $10^{-4}$ to 1. The same synthetic $X,y$ are solved
at $\lambda\in\{10^{-4},10^{-2},1\}$ with L-BFGS memory $\bar m=24$.
At the smallest $\lambda$, even the restricted Hessian condition number
is approximately $5\times10^7$, so this case tests more than the weak
null-space regularization of the ML-CUP25 sweep.

\noindent Figure~\ref{fig:comparison_stress} and
Table~\ref{tab:comparison_stress} show that L-BFGS converges in all three
cases and remains faster than QR. Its iteration count rises from 9 to 37
as regularization weakens, narrowing the timing advantage from about
$10.1$ to $1.7$. The forward-error curves almost overlap: both methods
reach approximately $2.7\times10^{-13}$ at $\lambda=10^{-4}$ and
$5.2\times10^{-15}$ at $\lambda=1$. These results use the configured memory of 24. All recorded L-BFGS
configurations in the notebook satisfy their requested tolerance.

\section{Reusing a Factorization for Multiple Right-Hand Sides}
\label{sec:comparison_multiple_rhs}

\noindent When $X$ and $\lambda$ remain fixed, QR can reuse $R$ and the
stored reflectors: each additional response requires only applying $Q^T$
and solving the triangular system. The notebook measures factorization,
a solve with reused factors, and a fresh L-BFGS run separately on one
deterministic response vector. It then projects the cost for $N$
right-hand sides (labelled $k$ in the figure) using
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:comparison_multiple_rhs}
    T_{\mathrm{QR}}(N)=t_{\mathrm{fact}}+N\,t_{\mathrm{solve}},
    \qquad
    T_{\mathrm{L\text{-}BFGS}}(N)=N\,t_{\mathrm{L\text{-}BFGS}}.
\end{equation}
These curves extrapolate the measured per-phase medians; they are not
end-to-end timings of batches of different response vectors. The L-BFGS
model assumes a fresh zero start and comparable work for each response,
without warm starts or reuse of curvature information.

\noindent The left panel of Figure~\ref{fig:comparison_multiple_rhs}
shows no timing crossover for this implementation. Factorization takes
$9.230$ ms, a solve with reused QR factors takes $2.093$ ms, and L-BFGS
takes $0.508$ ms. Since $t_{\mathrm{solve}}>t_{\mathrm{L\text{-}BFGS}}$,
the QR line has both a positive setup cost and a larger slope. Reuse alone
therefore cannot make it cheaper under this timing model. The reused QR
solution still agrees with the SVD reference to $3.35\times10^{-15}$,
so the comparison is not caused by a loss of accuracy.

\noindent The right panel reaches a different conclusion in operation
counts. The model gives $6{,}539{,}000$ flops for factorization,
$295{,}500$ per QR solve, and $882{,}000$ per L-BFGS solve for the observed
history. The break-even value is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:comparison_rhs_flops}
    N^*_{\mathrm{flops}}
    =\frac{6{,}539{,}000}{882{,}000-295{,}500}
    \approx11.1,
\end{equation}
so QR has the smaller modeled operation count from 12 right-hand sides
onwards. This advantage in arithmetic work does not translate into elapsed
time here: reflector application and back substitution retain Python loops,
whereas the L-BFGS matrix--vector operations use compiled numerical kernels.
The discrepancy illustrates why factorization reuse, flop counts, and
measured runtime must be considered separately.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:comparison_conclusions}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Small effective rank and increasing $m$.} L-BFGS is faster
    than the project QR solver while retaining comparable forward accuracy;
    its pair storage grows linearly with $m$. The thin-SVD library baseline
    is faster still on the tested tall, narrow matrices.
    \item \textbf{Increasing $n$.} Even with memory scaled to $n$, L-BFGS
    requires more iterations and loses its large timing advantage. QR has
    a slightly lower median from $n=200$ in this run and smaller forward
    errors over the larger-$n$ cases.
    \item \textbf{Conditioning and memory.} L-BFGS retains near-working-precision
    coefficient accuracy at small $\lambda$ on ML-CUP25, where structured QR
    loses forward accuracy despite a small gradient. With memory 24,
    L-BFGS also converges on every prescribed stress-test problem and remains
    faster, with forward errors comparable to QR.
    \item \textbf{Repeated right-hand sides.} Reusing QR factors reduces the
    modeled arithmetic cost enough to overtake fresh L-BFGS solves from
    12 responses, but the measured per-response times produce no crossover
    for the present Python implementations.
\end{itemize}
\noindent The choice between the two methods depends on the spectral
dimension, regularization, memory, and workload. The observed rankings
apply to these implementations and sampled problems; neither the ambient
dimension nor the full condition number alone determines the preferred
solver.

% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
% L-BFGS Experimental Results
\label{sec:experiments}
% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\noindent All experiments use the matrix $X \in \R^{m \times n}$ with $m = 500$
samples and $n = 12$ features after standard preprocessing (zero mean, unit
variance per feature). The response vector $y \in \R^n$ is drawn from a
standard normal distribution with a fixed random seed ($\texttt{seed} = 42$).
The reference solution $w^*$ is computed from the thin SVD of $X$,
$w^* = U\operatorname{diag}\bigl(\sigma_i/(\sigma_i^2+\lambda^2)\bigr)V^Ty$,
which does not form $XX^T$: the normal equations lose accuracy as $\lambda$
decreases (Section~\ref{sec:qr_stability}) and, on this problem, are less
accurate than L-BFGS itself (Section~\ref{sec:exp_lambda}).

\noindent Unless stated otherwise, we use the default configuration selected by
a grid search over the algorithm's hyperparameters on the base problem
(with the initial point fixed to $w_0 = 0$, the canonical neutral choice):
regularization $\lambda = 0.5$ (yielding $\kappa(\Xhat) = 151.49$), exact line
search, memory size $\bar{m} = 12$, equal to the rank $n$ of $XX^T$, the
Barzilai--Borwein $H_0$ scaling (\texttt{bb1}), and no curvature-based
restart. The grid search shows that, from $\bar m = 12$ upwards, the iteration
count is insensitive to the $H_0$ scaling and to the restart; these choices
only matter below the effective rank, where \texttt{bb1} without restart is
the most robust option (Sections~\ref{sec:exp_h0} and~\ref{sec:exp_restart}).
The relative tolerance is $\varepsilon = 10^{-14}$. All experiments are run on
a single machine; wall-clock measurements use \texttt{time.perf\_counter()}
and report the median over ten repetitions after a discarded warm-up run,
while FLOP counts use the operation model of Section~\ref{sec:exp_cost}.

\noindent The experimental discussion is organised in three parts. We first verify
the \emph{theoretical convergence properties} of
Section~\ref{sec:convergence} (convergence rates and finite termination,
Newton-direction alignment, and a comparison against steepest descent and
conjugate gradient). We then examine the \emph{algorithmic design choices}
of our L-BFGS variant (line search, Wolfe constants, memory size,
regularization, $H_0$ scaling, restart). Finally, we probe the
\emph{numerical and computational behaviour} of the method (sensitivity to
initialisation, theoretical and actual cost, and scalability in $n$ and $m$).

\section{Theoretical Convergence Properties}
\label{sec:exp_theory}
% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Empirical Convergence Rates and Finite Termination}
\label{sec:exp_rates}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\noindent We track the convergence behaviour at three memory sizes:
$\bar{m} = 3$ (below the effective rank), $\bar{m} = 12$ (the default), and
$\bar{m} = m = 500$ (effectively full BFGS, since the algorithm always
converges in fewer than $m$ iterations). We record two per-iteration
quantities: the Q-linear ratio
$r_k^{\mathrm{lin}} = (f_k - f^*)/(f_{k-1} - f^*)$, which the linear bound of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:linconv} keeps below a constant smaller than $1$, and the
Q-superlinear ratio
$r_k^{\mathrm{sup}} = \norm{w_{k+1} - w^*}/\norm{w_k - w^*}$, whose decay
towards $0$ is the signature of the superlinear regime of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:superlin}.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{L-BFGS at three memory sizes ($\lambda = 0.5$, exact LS,
  $\varepsilon = 10^{-14}$).}
\label{tab:rates}
\small
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{12pt}
\begin{tabular}{cccc}
    \toprule
    $\bar{m}$ & \textbf{Iterations} & $\norm{w - w^*}$ & $|f - f^*|$ \\
    \midrule
    3   & 15 & $2.19 \times 10^{-15}$ & $1.4 \times 10^{-17}$ \\
    12  & 12 & $2.20 \times 10^{-15}$ & $6.9 \times 10^{-17}$ \\
    500 & 12 & $2.20 \times 10^{-15}$ & $6.9 \times 10^{-17}$ \\
    \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{Chapters/images/convergence_ratios_plot.png}
    \caption{Q-linear (left) and Q-superlinear (right) convergence ratios
      ($\lambda = 0.5$, exact LS, $\varepsilon = 10^{-14}$). The dotted grey
      line in the left panel marks the steepest-descent bound
      $r_{\mathrm{SD}} \approx 0.9998$.}
    \label{fig:rates}
\end{figure}

\noindent The two larger memories, $\bar{m} = 12$ and $\bar{m} = 500$, produce
\emph{identical} trajectories and converge in $12$ iterations to an accuracy
of $2.2 \times 10^{-15}$. After the first iterations both ratios fluctuate
with a decreasing trend: the Q-linear one between about $10^{-1}$ and
$10^{-3}$, far below the steepest-descent bound
$r_{\mathrm{SD}} = \bigl((\kappa(H)-1)/(\kappa(H)+1)\bigr)^2 \approx 0.9998$,
with $\kappa(H) = \kappa(\Xhat)^2$, which is essentially $1$ on this problem;
the Q-superlinear one between about $0.3$ and $0.05$, without approaching
zero. The run then ends with an abrupt collapse of both ratios at the last
iteration, rather than a gradual superlinear decay. This is the signature of
the finite termination of Section~\ref{sec:finite_termination}: the Hessian
restricted to the range of $X$, where the iterates live, has $12$ distinct
eigenvalues, and once the method has explored the corresponding $12$
directions the solution is reached to machine precision. The values of
$|f - f^*|$, of order $10^{-17}$, are at the rounding level
$\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}} f^*$ of the objective ($f^* \approx 0.09$); the
computed differences are in fact slightly negative. The last point of the
left panel is therefore produced by flooring this difference at a small
positive constant before taking the ratio, so its vertical position carries
no information: only the collapse itself does. In the right panel, where
$\norm{w_k - w^*}$ stays positive throughout, the drop of about five orders
of magnitude at the final step is measured rather than imposed.

\noindent With the memory below the effective rank, $\bar{m} = 3$ follows the same
trajectory for the first iterations and then needs three more iterations
(15), reaching the same accuracy. Its ratios show isolated returns close to
or slightly above $1$, i.e.\ steps of little progress, or steps that
temporarily move the iterate away from $w^*$, where the three stored pairs
cannot represent the curvature directions explored earlier. A memory
comparable to the effective rank therefore matches the full-memory method
\emph{exactly}: by Lemma~\ref{lemma:singvals}, $XX^T$ has only $n = 12$
non-zero eigenvalues, so storing $12$ vectors is as good as storing all
$500$.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Comparison with Steepest Descent and Conjugate Gradient}
\label{sec:exp_baselines}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\noindent To assess the practical value of the curvature information used by
L-BFGS, we compare it with two natural baselines: \emph{steepest descent}
(SD) with exact line search, and the linear \emph{conjugate gradient} method
(CG) applied to the symmetric positive definite (SPD) system $Hw = Xy$ through
implicit products $Hv = X(X^Tv) + \lambda^2 v$, with the same per-iteration
cost as L-BFGS. All methods start from $w_0 = 0$ with
$\varepsilon = 10^{-14}$, in a well-conditioned ($\lambda = 1$,
$\kappa \approx 76$) and an ill-conditioned ($\lambda = 10^{-4}$,
$\kappa \approx 7.6 \times 10^5$) regime.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Iteration counts and final gradient norms across four methods
  ($\varepsilon = 10^{-14}$, exact LS where applicable).}
\label{tab:baselines}
\small
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{10pt}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
    \toprule
    & \multicolumn{2}{c}{\emph{Well-cond.} ($\lambda = 1$)}
    & \multicolumn{2}{c}{\emph{Ill-cond.} ($\lambda = 10^{-4}$)} \\
    \cmidrule(lr){2-3}\cmidrule(lr){4-5}
    \textbf{Method} & iter & final $\norm{\nabla f}$ & iter & final $\norm{\nabla f}$ \\
    \midrule
    Steepest descent        & 1447 & $6.14\times10^{-13}$ & 1725 & $6.55\times10^{-13}$ \\
    Conjugate gradient      & 15   & $5.47\times10^{-15}$ & 15   & $1.90\times10^{-14}$ \\
    L-BFGS ($\bar{m} = 12$) & 12   & $5.98\times10^{-14}$ & 12   & $6.61\times10^{-14}$ \\
    Full BFGS ($\bar{m} = 500$) & 12 & $5.98\times10^{-14}$ & 12 & $6.61\times10^{-14}$ \\
    \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{Chapters/images/comparison_sd_cg.png}
    \caption{Convergence of the four methods ($\norm{\nabla f}$ vs.\
      iteration) in the well-conditioned (left) and ill-conditioned (right)
      regimes. L-BFGS and full BFGS overlap; CG follows them for the first
      iterations and then lags behind. The SD curve is truncated at $60$
      iterations; its full length is reported in
      Table~\ref{tab:baselines}.}
    \label{fig:baselines}
\end{figure}

\noindent Steepest descent is uncompetitive: using only the gradient, it needs
over $1400$ iterations in both regimes, zigzagging across the elongated level
sets; its iteration count grows with $\kappa$, from $1447$ to $1725$. L-BFGS
instead needs $12$ iterations in \emph{both} regimes, for $\bar m = 12$ and
full BFGS alike: its iteration count does not depend on $\kappa$, in
agreement with the finite-termination argument of
Section~\ref{sec:finite_termination}.

\noindent In exact arithmetic CG would terminate after the same $12$ steps,
since the Hessian restricted to the range of $X$ has $12$ distinct
eigenvalues~\cite[Theorem~5.4]{nocedal2006numerical}. In practice it needs
$15$, and checking the \emph{true} gradient at each iterate, rather than the
residual updated by recurrence, gives exactly the same counts for every $n$: the difference is not
due to the stopping test. Figure~\ref{fig:baselines} shows what happens: CG
and L-BFGS produce the same gradient norms for the first iterations, as
expected from their close relation on quadratics
(Section~\ref{sec:finite_termination}), and then CG drifts away. Rounding
errors make the CG directions progressively lose conjugacy and delay
convergence, whereas L-BFGS stores the curvature pairs explicitly and reaches
the theoretical count. The final gradient norm of CG is lower than that of
L-BFGS, but this reflects the three extra iterations it performs rather than
a more accurate solution. On non-quadratic objectives, where CG loses its
conjugacy guarantees altogether, the advantage of L-BFGS would be even
larger.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Alignment with the Newton Direction}
\label{sec:exp_newton}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\noindent To explain the behaviour of the convergence ratios, we measure how
closely the L-BFGS direction $p_k = -H_k\nabla f_k$ approximates the Newton
direction $p_k^{\mathrm{N}} = -H^{-1}\nabla f_k$ through the alignment defect
$1 - \cos\theta_k$, with
$\cos\theta_k = \langle p_k, p_k^{\mathrm{N}}\rangle /
(\norm{p_k}\,\norm{p_k^{\mathrm{N}}})$. The Newton direction is computed from
a cached Cholesky factorisation of $H$ and used \emph{only as a measurement
oracle}, not by the algorithm. The series are truncated once
$\norm{\nabla f_k} < 10^{-9}$, below which both directions are computed on
numerical noise.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Alignment defect $1 - \cos\theta_k$ at the last \emph{clean}
  iteration ($\norm{\nabla f_k} \geq 10^{-9}$), $\lambda = 0.5$, exact LS.}
\label{tab:newton_angle}
\small
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{12pt}
\begin{tabular}{cccc}
    \toprule
    $\bar{m}$ & \textbf{Iterations} & \textbf{Clean iterations}
      & \textbf{Final} $1 - \cos\theta_k$ \\
    \midrule
    3   & 15 & 15 & $2.50 \times 10^{-8}$  \\
    12  & 12 & 12 & $2.55 \times 10^{-14}$ \\
    500 & 12 & 12 & $2.55 \times 10^{-14}$ \\
    \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent With $\bar{m} = 12$ and full BFGS the defect falls to
$2.6 \times 10^{-14}$, i.e.\ to machine precision: by the end of the run the
L-BFGS direction \emph{is} the Newton direction, which is why the last step
reaches the solution. Even $\bar{m} = 3$ aligns closely, to
$2.5 \times 10^{-8}$: a memory below the effective rank limits how accurately
$H_k$ can reproduce $H^{-1}$, which explains the few extra iterations, but
the direction remains strongly Newton-like. The quality of the Newton
approximation built by the two-loop recursion thus grows with the memory and
saturates once the memory reaches the effective rank.

\section{Algorithmic Design Choices}
\label{sec:exp_design}
% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\noindent We now examine the design choices of our implementation: the line
search, the Wolfe constants, the memory size, the regularization parameter,
the $H_0$ scaling and the curvature-based restart. Each experiment varies one
choice and keeps the others at their default value.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Exact Line Search vs.\ Strong Wolfe Line Search}
\label{sec:exp_exact_vs_wolfe}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\noindent We compare the two line-search strategies with the standard Wolfe
constants $(c_1, c_2) = (10^{-4}, 0.9)$ recommended by Nocedal and
Wright~\cite[Sec.~3.1]{nocedal2006numerical} for quasi-Newton methods.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Comparison of line-search strategies
  ($m = 500$, $n = 12$, $\lambda = 0.5$, $\bar{m} = 12$,
  $\varepsilon = 10^{-14}$).}
\label{tab:ls_comparison}
\small
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{10pt}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Line Search} & \textbf{Iterations} & $\norm{w - w^*}$
      & \textbf{Time (ms)} & \textbf{Stop reason} \\
    \midrule
    Exact   & 12  & $2.20 \times 10^{-15}$ & 0.44 & tolerance reached \\
    Wolfe ($c_1=10^{-4}, c_2=0.9$) & 30 & $1.43 \times 10^{-9}$ & 1.73 & stagnation \\
    \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent Exact line search dominates: it converges in $12$ iterations against
$30$, reaches an accuracy six orders of magnitude higher, and is about
$4\times$ faster in wall-clock time. Its closed-form step,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:exact_alpha_expanded}, exploits the quadratic structure exactly
at the cost of a single matrix-vector product, whereas each Wolfe iteration
needs several function and gradient evaluations.

\noindent The Wolfe variant does not diverge, but stops on stagnation at
$\norm{\nabla f} \approx 2 \times 10^{-8}$, without reaching the tolerance.
This is an intrinsic limit of line searches based on function values: with
such a small gradient, the decrease of $f$ required by the Armijo
condition~\eqref{eq:armijo} falls below the precision with which
$f \approx 0.09$ can be computed, about
$\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}} f \approx 2 \times 10^{-17}$, so the line search
can no longer verify it. One iteration before stopping, the run also triggers
the negative-curvature reset of Section~\ref{sec:restart}: the computed
$y_k^T s_k$ is exactly zero, so the pair carries no usable curvature and the
memory is cleared. Both symptoms have the same origin, the loss of
significance in quantities derived from $f$ near the solution. The exact line
search never compares function values and has no such limit.

\noindent The constant $c_1$ has no effect: all three values give identical
results, because the Armijo condition is already satisfied at the trial step
selected by the curvature condition. The curvature constant $c_2$ matters
more, and strict values work best: $c_2 = 0.1$ needs $16$ iterations, the

need $26$--$30$. A small $c_2$ forces the accepted step close to the
minimiser along $p_k$, i.e.\ close to the exact step. It costs more
line-search evaluations per iteration ($5.7$ on average at $c_2 = 0.1$,
against $3.3$--$4.0$ for $c_2 \geq 0.5$), but needs fewer iterations, so its
total number of evaluations is still the lowest ($91$). In all cases the
final accuracy is limited by the stagnation of
Section~\ref{sec:exp_exact_vs_wolfe}, to $10^{-10}$--$10^{-9}$. The standard
value $c_2 = 0.9$, designed for general quasi-Newton problems, is therefore
not the best choice on this quadratic, and exact line search remains
preferable whenever it is available in closed form.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering

    \caption{Gradient-norm convergence for $\bar{m} \in \{1, 2, 3, 5, 10, 12,

\noindent The iteration count decreases \emph{monotonically} with the memory, from
$20$ iterations with a single pair to $12$ at $\bar m = 12$, and then
saturates. The saturation point is exactly the effective rank $n = 12$: once
the stored pairs span the curvature directions of the Hessian restricted to
the range of $X$, additional pairs carry no new information, consistently
with Sections~\ref{sec:exp_rates} and~\ref{sec:exp_newton}.
Figure~\ref{fig:memory_effect} shows how this happens: all memories, even
$\bar m = 1$, follow the same trajectory for the first iterations, and the
smaller memories leave it one after another, between iterations $7$ and $12$
--- the smaller the memory, the earlier the departure. This agrees with the
classical result that memoryless BFGS, i.e.\ a single stored pair with
$H_0 = I$, combined with an exact line search coincides with the
Hestenes--Stiefel conjugate gradient
method~\cite[Sec.~9.1]{nocedal2006numerical}: in exact arithmetic even a
minimal memory would follow the CG trajectory. In practice, rounding errors
and the rescaling of $H_k^0 = \gamma_k I$ at every iteration break this
equivalence, and a small memory, which relies on the implicit conjugacy of
the directions rather than on stored curvature pairs, is affected first.

\noindent Even a very small memory converges reliably, and to the same accuracy,
of order $10^{-15}$ (slightly higher, $5 \times 10^{-14}$, with $\bar m = 1$).
This is consistent with the observation of Nocedal and
Wright~\cite[Sec.~9.1]{nocedal2006numerical} that modest memories often give
satisfactory results. The wall-clock time stays between $0.41$ and
$0.49$\,ms for every memory size: on this problem the differences of a few
iterations are comparable to the measurement noise. The practical
recommendation is to set $\bar{m}$ equal to the effective rank: smaller
values cost a few extra iterations, larger values only add storage.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Effect of the Regularization Parameter $\lambda$}
\label{sec:exp_lambda}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\noindent We study convergence across five regularization regimes. Recall from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:condition} that $\kappa(\Xhat)$ scales as $O(1/\lambda)$ for
small $\lambda$, while large $\lambda$ drives $\kappa(\Xhat) \to 1$, making
the problem nearly spherical. The last column of Table~\ref{tab:lambda_effect}
reports the distance between the normal-equations solution and the SVD
reference.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Effect of $\lambda$ on condition number and convergence
  ($m = 500$, $n = 12$, exact LS, $\bar{m} = 12$, $\varepsilon = 10^{-14}$;
  $w^*$ from the SVD reference).}
\label{tab:lambda_effect}
\small
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{8pt}
\begin{tabular}{lccccc}
    \toprule
    $\lambda$ & $\kappa(\Xhat)$ & \textbf{Iter}
      & $\norm{w - w^*}$ & $t_{\mathrm{LBFGS}}$ (ms)
      & $\norm{w_{\mathrm{NE}} - w^*}$ \\
    \midrule
    $10^{-4}$ & $7.6 \times 10^{5}$ & 12 & $2.23 \times 10^{-15}$ & 0.42 & $1.11 \times 10^{-5}$  \\
    $10^{-2}$ & $7.6 \times 10^{3}$ & 12 & $2.26 \times 10^{-15}$ & 0.41 & $1.11 \times 10^{-9}$  \\
    $1$       & $75.8$              & 12 & $1.96 \times 10^{-15}$ & 0.41 & $1.03 \times 10^{-13}$ \\
    $10^{2}$  & $1.3$               &  6 & $1.48 \times 10^{-17}$ & 0.16 & $1.52 \times 10^{-17}$ \\

    \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent Neither the iteration count nor the accuracy of L-BFGS depends on the
conditioning. For every $\lambda \leq 1$, from $\kappa \approx 76$ to
$\kappa \approx 7.6 \times 10^5$, L-BFGS needs exactly $12$ iterations and
reaches $\norm{w - w^*} \approx 2 \times 10^{-15}$. Both facts have the same
structural reason. Starting from $w_0 = 0$, all iterates remain in the range
of $X$, where the Hessian has the $n$ eigenvalues $\sigma_i^2 + \lambda^2$:
there are $12$ of them whatever $\lambda$ is, which fixes the iteration count
(Section~\ref{sec:finite_termination}), and their ratio
$(\sigma_{\max}^2+\lambda^2)/(\sigma_{\min}^2+\lambda^2)$ stays bounded as
$\lambda \to 0$, because $\sigma_{\min}(X) > 0$, which preserves the
accuracy. L-BFGS never forms $XX^T$ and never sees the full condition number.

\noindent The normal equations behave very differently: their distance from the
SVD reference grows from $10^{-13}$ at $\lambda = 1$ to $1.1 \times 10^{-5}$
at $\lambda = 10^{-4}$, following the squared condition number of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:Xhat_gram} (Section~\ref{sec:qr_stability}). At small $\lambda$
L-BFGS is therefore far more accurate than a reference computed from the
normal equations, which is why all experiments use the SVD reference.

\noindent In the regime $\lambda \gg \sigma_{\max}(X)$ the regularization term
dominates, $H \approx \lambda^2 I$, and L-BFGS converges in $3$--$6$
iterations on a nearly spherical problem. Across all regimes L-BFGS runs in
under $0.5$\,ms, and the wall-clock time follows the iteration count.

\noindent This experiment also clarifies why we adopt $\lambda = 0.5$ as the
default. The parameter $\lambda$ is not an algorithmic knob but part of the
problem definition: it sets the trade-off between fitting the data and
controlling $\norm{w}$. Although large $\lambda$ makes the problem
numerically trivial, the resulting solution is dominated by the penalty term
and is essentially $w \approx 0$, ignoring the data. The value
$\lambda = 0.5$ yields a moderately ill-conditioned instance
($\kappa \approx 151$) that is representative of realistic regularization.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{$H_0$ Scaling Strategies}
\label{sec:exp_h0}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\noindent We compare the three strategies of Section~\ref{sec:h0_scaling} for the

restart, \texttt{bb1} needs $14$ and $15$ iterations, against $20$ and $23$ for

$10^{-13}$--$3 \times 10^{-14}$). By the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality
$\gamma^{\mathrm{BB1}} \geq \gamma^{\mathrm{N}}$: when the few stored pairs
carry little curvature information, the larger initial scale produces more
effective steps. This robustness is why \texttt{bb1} is the default. The
restart helps \texttt{nocedal} at small memory ($20 \to 15$ and
$23 \to 18$ iterations) but brings no benefit to \texttt{bb1}, which already
performs as well without it.

iterates in every configuration. The safeguard clips $\gamma$ into a safe
interval to guard against numerical degeneration near the solution, where it
becomes the ratio of two vanishing quantities; on this quadratic $\gamma$
never approaches the clipping bounds, so the safeguard never activates.

\section{Numerical and Computational Scalability}
\label{sec:exp_scalability}
% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\noindent The previous sections characterised the convergence of L-BFGS on the
default ML-CUP instance. We now probe three remaining practical aspects: the
dependence on the starting point, the computational cost and storage, and
the behaviour as the problem dimensions $n$ and $m$ change.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\noindent Theory guarantees convergence from any starting point on a strongly
convex quadratic. We test four starting points spanning four orders of
magnitude in $\norm{w_0 - w^*}$: the zero vector, two random Gaussian vectors

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Theoretical and Actual Cost, Storage}
\label{sec:exp_cost}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\noindent We make the cost of L-BFGS concrete with a FLOP count derived from the
structure of the algorithm, counting one flop per floating-point addition or
multiplication as in~\cite{golub2013}. With a full memory of $\bar{m}$
pairs, one iteration costs
\[
  \text{FLOPs/iter} = \underbrace{(8\bar{m} + 1)\,m}_{\text{two-loop}}
                    + \underbrace{4mn}_{\text{gradient}}
                    + \underbrace{2mn + 4m}_{\text{exact LS}}
                    + \underbrace{2mn}_{\text{objective}}
                    + \underbrace{2m}_{\text{update}},
\]
where the objective term is the evaluation of $f$ used by the stagnation
check. This is an upper bound: while the memory fills up, and after a reset,
the two-loop recursion works on fewer pairs. We therefore also record the
number of pairs actually stored at each iteration and compute the flops
actually performed. Storage is $(2\bar{m} + 4)\,m$ floats: $2\bar m$ vectors
of length $m$ for the curvature pairs and four working vectors.

\noindent The actual cost is minimal and constant from $\bar m = 12$ onwards,

shown in Section~\ref{sec:exp_scaling_nm}, the iteration count does not grow
with $m$. The direct solver considered here is the dense solution of the
normal equations, whose cost grows as $O(m^3)$; the structure-based QR of
Chapter~5 reduces the direct cost to $O(nm^2)$, and the two approaches are
compared in Chapter~7.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Empirical Scaling with the Dimensions $n$ and $m$}
\label{sec:exp_scaling_nm}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\noindent All previous experiments use the fixed ML-CUP matrix ($m = 500$,
$n = 12$). To separate the effect of the dimensions from that of the
conditioning, we run two sweeps on synthetic data with a prescribed spectrum:
$X = U \Sigma V^T$, where $U$ and $V$ are the orthonormal factors of a
Gaussian matrix and the singular values are geometrically spaced from
$\sigma_{\max}$ down to $\sigma_{\max}/\rho$. Since
$\kappa(\Xhat) = \sqrt{\sigma_{\max}^2 + \lambda^2}/\lambda$ by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:condition}, fixing $\sigma_{\max}$ and $\lambda$ makes the
condition number \emph{identical} on every row of both sweeps. We set
$\sigma_{\max} = 75.74$ and $\lambda = 0.5$, the values of the ML-CUP
instance, so that $\kappa(\Xhat) = 151.5$ throughout, and $\rho = 20$.
Prescribing the spectrum means the columns are no longer standardised as in
the real data: this is a deliberate trade-off, since controlling $\kappa$ is
what makes the effect of the dimension readable. For the $n$-sweep we also
record the saturation memory $\bar m^*$, the smallest memory reaching the
minimum iteration count, obtained by testing every $\bar m$ from $1$ to
$n + 10$.

\noindent Varying $m$ at fixed $n$ and fixed conditioning, the iteration count of
L-BFGS is constant at $12$ across a forty-fold increase in the sample size,
from $m = 200$ to $m = 8000$. Convergence is therefore governed by the
effective rank $n$ and is entirely independent of the ambient dimension $m$;
combined with a per-iteration cost linear in $m$
(Section~\ref{sec:exp_cost}), the total work of L-BFGS grows only linearly
with the number of samples.

\noindent The comparison with CG is the one result that changes qualitatively
with the dimension. On the ML-CUP instance CG needed $15$ iterations against
the $12$ of L-BFGS (Section~\ref{sec:exp_baselines}), a gap of three steps.
Here the gap widens monotonically with $n$: $17$ against $12$, $40$ against
$25$, $93$ against $50$, and $182$ against $93$, so that at $n = 100$ CG
performs almost twice as many iterations and exceeds by a factor $1.8$ the
$n$ steps it would need in exact arithmetic. As in Section~\ref{sec:exp_baselines}, this is not an artefact of the
stopping test: checking the true gradient at each iterate rather than the
residual updated by recurrence gives exactly the same counts for every $n$. The
explanation is the same erosion of conjugacy by rounding errors, but its
effect is far more visible here than on the ML-CUP instance, because the
prescribed geometric spectrum spans two orders of magnitude and the Krylov
directions become numerically dependent much earlier. L-BFGS, which stores
the curvature pairs explicitly instead of relying on the implicit conjugacy
of the recurrence, is unaffected and stays close to the theoretical count. The
modest advantage observed on the base problem is therefore not a peculiarity
of that instance: it is the small-$n$ end of a gap that grows with the
effective rank.


% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
\section{Summary}
\label{sec:exp_summary}
% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\noindent Our experimental results confirm the theoretical analysis of
Section~\ref{sec:convergence} and provide several practical insights into
the design of L-BFGS for this problem class.

\noindent On the \emph{theoretical} side, the most important observation is that
the convergence of L-BFGS on our problem is governed by the effective rank of
$XX^T$, not by the condition number. With a memory at least equal to the
effective rank, L-BFGS converges in exactly $n = 12$ iterations in every
regime, from $\kappa \approx 76$ to $\kappa \approx 7.6 \times 10^5$, to an
accuracy of order $10^{-15}$, ending with the abrupt collapse that signals
finite termination; its direction becomes the Newton direction to machine
precision. Steepest descent needs over $1400$ iterations and slows down as
$\kappa$ grows, while CG, which would terminate in the same $12$ steps in
exact arithmetic, needs $15$ because rounding errors erode the conjugacy of
its directions.

\noindent On the \emph{algorithmic} side, exact line search dominates the strong
Wolfe variant ($12$ iterations against $30$, and about $4\times$ faster), whose
accuracy is limited by its reliance on function values; among the Wolfe
constants, only a strict $c_2$ brings it close to the exact step. The memory
improves convergence monotonically up to the effective rank and then
saturates, so $\bar m = n$ is the natural choice. With such a memory the
$H_0$ scaling and the restart do not matter; below the effective rank,
\texttt{bb1} is the most robust scaling and the restart brings no benefit,
which motivates the default configuration. These conclusions hold in
wall-clock time as well as in iterations.

\noindent On the \emph{numerical} side, L-BFGS is insensitive to the starting
point and far more accurate than the normal equations at small $\lambda$,
because its iterates stay in the range of $X$ and it never forms $XX^T$. Its
actual cost is constant for every memory beyond the effective rank, whereas a
full-memory FLOP model overestimates it by up to $2.9\times$, and its storage
is negligible. Varying the dimensions with the conditioning held fixed
confirms both halves of this picture directly: the iteration count equals the
effective rank $n$ and is constant at $12$ from $m = 200$ to $m = 8000$, so
the total work grows only linearly with the number of samples, while the
memory needed tracks $n$ from just below it. The same sweep shows that the
advantage of L-BFGS over CG, three iterations on the base problem, widens
with $n$ up to almost a factor two at $n = 100$, as rounding errors erode the
conjugacy of the CG directions.
